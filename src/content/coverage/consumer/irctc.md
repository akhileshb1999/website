• IRCTC's monopoly is real but its economics are the opposite of what the revenue mix suggests. Internet ticketing accounts for just over 26% of quarterly revenue yet runs at an 85% operating margin, while catering contributes roughly 46% of revenue at a 10% margin. The company therefore earns most of its profit from a minority of its revenue, and the ticketing franchise — through which around 89% of India's reserved rail tickets are booked — is effectively the entire investment case.

• This mix asymmetry means revenue growth can actively destroy margin. FY26 EBITDA margin declined to ~32%, with Q4 at 27.3%, precisely because the faster-growing segments were the lower-margin ones: tourism grew 29% at a 19% margin while catering expanded its share of the mix, and Rail Neer grew 6.5% at 15%. Growth in catering and tourism raises the topline and lowers the margin simultaneously, so segment mix matters more here than the consolidated growth rate [internet ticketing share of EBIT FY26: __%; FY29E EBITDA margin: __%].

• The binding constraint on the profitable segment is regulatory rather than commercial. Convenience fee is set between ₹15 and ₹40 per ticket by policy, not by the company, so monetisation of a genuine monopoly is capped by the government that owns the majority of it. Volume growth is therefore the only lever available on the high-margin business, which makes rail passenger growth and digitisation of remaining offline bookings the variables that matter [reserved ticket volume growth FY27E-29E: __%; convenience fee revision: __].

• Key risk: 1) Any revision or withdrawal of the convenience fee, which has precedent and would strike directly at the segment generating most of the profit 2) Continued mix shift toward catering and tourism diluting consolidated margins even as revenue grows.
