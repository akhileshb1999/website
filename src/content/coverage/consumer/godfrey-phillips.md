• Godfrey Phillips is the number two player in Indian cigarettes with roughly 14% of the domestic market against ITC's ~73%, and the same structural conditions apply — advertising is banned, states capture the majority of retail price, and volumes are governed by taxation rather than competition. Being the challenger in a category with an advertising ban is normally a permanent disadvantage, which is why the Marlboro licence matters disproportionately: it provides the one internationally established premium brand in India that its larger competitor cannot replicate.

• The gap between gross sales value of ₹18,379cr and net profit of ₹1,525cr in FY26 illustrates the industry's structure directly — the overwhelming majority of what the consumer pays is excise, GST and state levies rather than manufacturer economics. What the company controls is mix and cost, and Q3 FY26 showed revenue growing 15.6% while profit rose 9%, the same pass-through lag on higher levies visible at ITC [FY29E EBITDA margin: __%].

• As the smaller player, the arithmetic of share gain works in Godfrey's favour: each point of category share taken is worth roughly five times more to a 14% player than to a 73% one, and the company has been expanding direct retail coverage to that end. The GST move from 28% to a flat 40% in February 2026 is the immediate test, and a more premium-weighted mix may absorb pricing better than the mass end of the market [domestic cigarette share FY26: ~14% -> FY29E: __%; FY27E volume growth: __%].

• Key risk: 1) Taxation-driven migration to illicit trade, which at 26.1% of the market damages the legal industry regardless of share position 2) Dependence on the Marlboro licence, an asset the company does not own and which is renewed on terms set by another party.
