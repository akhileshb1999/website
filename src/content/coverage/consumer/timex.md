• Timex demonstrates that the smartwatch did not kill the traditional watch so much as split the category in two. Functional timekeeping migrated to wearables, leaving analogue watches to compete as fashion accessories and small jewellery — a use case where design, brand and price point matter and battery life does not. Timex plays this through a licensed portfolio (Guess, Versace) alongside its own brand, buying established brand equity without the cost of building it, at the price of royalties and finite licence terms.

• The financial story is operating leverage on a small fixed base: revenue scaled from ₹265cr in FY22 to ₹800cr in FY26, a 32% CAGR, while EBITDA margin expanded from 9.2% to 14.5% — a 530bps move in a single year, with EBITDA up 134% on revenue up 48%. That asymmetry is the signature of a business whose cost base was built for a much smaller revenue line, and it implies the same sensitivity in reverse if growth stalls [FY29E EBITDA margin: __%].

• Growth is currently broad-based rather than dependent on one brand — the Timex brand itself grew 62% in FY26 with Guess and Versace in double digits — but the absolute base remains small at ₹800cr, which cuts both ways: percentage growth is easier to deliver, and any single distribution relationship or licence outcome moves the whole business [revenue CAGR FY26-29E: __%; licensed brands share of revenue: __%].

• Key risk: 1) Licence dependence, where Guess and Versace are assets the company does not own and renews on terms set by others 2) The operating leverage reversing sharply, given how much of recent profit growth came from margin expansion rather than volume alone.
